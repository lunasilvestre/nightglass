# INTSUM 2026/058 — Traffic Patterns at the Tagus Entrance

## 1. Operational geography

Area of interest **LISBON** covers the Tagus entrance, the port of Lisbon, the
Cascais and Trafaria anchorages, the approaches to Setúbal and a sizeable band
of open Atlantic to the west.

The access channel is narrow and well marked. Any vessel of size entering or
leaving the Tagus passes through a predictable corridor — which makes absences
in that corridor particularly informative, because AIS reception coverage there
is good and there is no range-based explanation for the silence.

## 2. Daily rhythm

- **Small hours and early morning.** Deep-sea arrivals waiting for high water or
  a pilotage window. The Cascais anchorage is typically occupied.
- **Mid-morning to late afternoon.** Heaviest movement, with cargo departures
  and short-sea traffic.
- **Night.** North–south transits offshore, not calling. These cross the box
  with no interaction with the port at all.

The Sentinel-1 descending pass (06:33–06:51 UTC) catches the end of the
anchorage period; the ascending pass (18:26–18:43 UTC) catches the end of the
busiest period. Neither samples still water.

## 3. Areas needing care

- **Cascais anchorage.** Long stays are normal and expected. What is not normal
  is a long stay with no subsequent port call.
- **Western band of the box, offshore.** Outside reliable coastal station range.
  Absences here are weakly informative.
- **Setúbal approaches.** Cargo and fishing traffic overlaid, with very
  different length populations in the same water.

## 4. Seasonality

Recreational craft increase sharply in summer, concentrated near the entrance
and off Cascais. Almost all of it is Class B or carries no AIS at all. The
practical effect is a seasonal rise in small unpaired detections that
corresponds to **no** change in behaviour — it is fleet composition, not
conduct.

A rise in the number of dark detections between June and September should, by
default, be attributed to this cause until shown otherwise.
